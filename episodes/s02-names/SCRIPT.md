## Fact checks (Nov 26, 2026)
- **Next-in-line effect:** Brenner (1973). Participants took turns reading words aloud; words read within about
  9 seconds before a participant's own turn were recalled worse than other words. Later work attributes it to an
  encoding deficit (attention goes to preparing one's own turn). Applying it to name introductions is our framing,
  so the narration says "the same thing" only implicitly; the on-screen fact is the study's own finding.
- **Production effect (fix):** MacLeod, Gopie, Hourihan, Neary & Ozubko (2010), *Journal of Experimental Psychology:
  Learning, Memory, and Cognition* 36, 671–685: reading words aloud gave better memory than reading them silently.
  Supported by meta-analyses (Fawcett, 2013; Bodner, Taikh & Fawcett, 2014). The card claims only "said out loud >
  read silently"; it does not claim that saying names back was tested specifically.
- **Considered and dropped:** the "doorway effect" (forgetting why you walked into a room). The original study found
  walking back to the first room did not restore memory, and a 2021 replication series found no significant effect,
  so it fails the one-verified-fact rule.
